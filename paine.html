PAiNe.

Planted Anomalies in Networks

Finding hidden structures in complex networks.

How can we distinguish genuinely anomalous patterns from structures that arise naturally through randomness? PAiNe explores when hidden structures can be detected, when their vertices can be recovered, and what efficient algorithms can achieve.

Three perspectives on one fundamental problem.

01 / THE PROBLEM

The Scientific Challenge.

When is a hidden structure truly anomalous?

Randomness can produce surprisingly organized patterns. Dense groups, highly connected hubs, and localized communities may emerge naturally in complex networks, even without external intervention.

How can we distinguish these spontaneous structures from anomalies deliberately embedded in a network?

PAiNe investigates the fundamental limits of detecting and recovering planted anomalies in random graphs. The surrounding network's geometry, heterogeneous connectivity and structural dependencies can make the same hidden structure easier—or harder—to identify.

DETECTIONIs something unusual present?

Determine whether a network contains a planted structure.

RECOVERYWhich vertices form that structure?

Identify the constituent vertices, possibly with partial or exact accuracy.

02 / RESEARCH GOALS

Research Objectives.

Four objectives, one central question.

01

Modeling planted anomalies

Investigate hidden structures in random graph models that capture realistic network features, including heterogeneous connectivity and geometry.

02

Fundamental recovery limits

Understand when anomalies can be detected or recovered, and how network structure shapes statistical thresholds and phase transitions.

03

Efficient recovery algorithms

Develop recovery methods with rigorous guarantees, connecting statistical accuracy with computational efficiency.

04

Empirical validation

Explore theoretical predictions through computational experiments and open, reproducible tools for network analysis.

03 / METHODOLOGY

Research Approach.

From a network model to a testable recovery method.

The strategy follows three connected checkpoints. We first create a controlled comparison between random networks with and without a planted anomaly; then use probabilistic arguments to identify where the two become distinguishable; finally, we turn that insight into recovery procedures and test their performance. The process can be revisited as new structural effects emerge.
